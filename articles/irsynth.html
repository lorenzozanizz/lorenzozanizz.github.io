Synthetic thermography in Blender: building IR-synth

On GitHub

This article discusses and uses code from a repository.

ir-synth ↗

Thermal cameras see heat instead of light. They work in the dark, through light smoke, and they show things that a normal camera cannot show. That makes them useful for detection, inspection, and navigation. It also makes training data hard to get. A thermal camera, a real scene, and a lot of careful labeling are all needed, and the result only fits one sensor in one set of conditions. One recent paper describes collecting a thermal dataset with a drone camera as a large effort: 6,664 images over 20 scenes, and the authors call it the first dataset of its kind for thermal novel view synthesis [1].

IR-synth is my attempt at a different route. It is a Blender add-on that produces synthetic thermal images from a normal 3D scene [14]. In the Blender sidebar the add-on is called Thermography, and internally it is named bl_thermal. You give each object a temperature and an emissivity. The add-on turns them into a signal that a thermal sensor would record, and Blender renders it. This article explains why the problem is harder than it looks, what other people do about it, and how IR-synth is built.

The project is at version 0.9.0 and it targets Blender 4.5 or later. Some parts are finished and some are placeholders. I will say clearly which is which.

The problem: Blender renders light, not heat

A thermal camera does not measure temperature directly. It measures the radiated power that reaches the detector inside a limited band of wavelengths, for example the long wave infrared band around 10 micrometers. The camera software then converts that power into a number. Two facts make this more than a simple color mapping.

First, a real surface radiates less than an ideal black body at the same temperature. The fraction it does radiate is its emissivity. Second, whatever is not emitted is mostly reflected. A shiny metal pot at 20 °C can look hot or cold in a thermal image, depending on what it reflects. The apparent signal is a mix of the surface itself and its surroundings.

Blender is built for visible light. Its renderer takes light sources, material colors, and three color channels. Temperature is not a quantity in a Blender scene. To get a thermal image, you have to bring temperature in, convert it to band radiance, and let the renderer handle the reflections, while keeping the renderer from "improving" the pixel values with tone mapping or denoising.

Many projects already use Blender for thermal data, and most take a practical shortcut. One drone detection pipeline turns generated backgrounds into grayscale and adds a light emission component to the drone models [2]. The authors note that the background generator they used was designed for visible images, not for accurate long wave infrared simulation. Another project uses real thermal backgrounds and builds a shader node network by hand, using the Fresnel node to change the apparent emissivity with the viewing angle [3]. The node setup is tuned by eye against real images.

These methods work for many detection tasks. But the images carry no temperature. A pixel is a gray level that someone chose, and it cannot be converted back to Kelvin. This also makes it hard to vary the scene in a controlled way, for example by changing only the ambient temperature.

State of the art

I looked at three families of methods. They give different answers to the same question.

Physics-based signature simulators

The oldest family comes from defense work. MuSES, from ThermoAnalytics, solves the heat balance for every facet of a scene and then predicts the radiance that a sensor would see. The vendor describes its renderer as using multi-bounce ray tracing, and its CoTherm tool automates the variation of input parameters to produce large image sets for machine learning [4]. DIRSIG follows the same chain: a thermodynamic model gives temperatures, and a ray tracer carries the radiation from the source to the target and to the sensor. A validation study of DIRSIG found that most of the error comes from the two big parts, the temperature predictor and the radiative transfer model, and it measured an average radiance error of about 14 percent on one real scene [5].

This family is the most accurate, and it also shows what is hard. Good thermal images need good temperatures. These tools are specialized, often closed, and aimed at expert users.

Blender as a radiometric layer

A second family uses Blender as the renderer and puts a separate thermal model in front of it. A group at Politecnico di Milano builds thermal images of spacecraft and asteroids this way. A finite volume thermal model gives the temperatures, Blender renders the scene from the camera position, and then thermal camera models are applied [6]. A 2026 conference talk from the University of Antwerp describes the same idea for thermography experiments: finite element or analytical models provide the temperature field, and Blender handles geometry, material optics, camera configuration, and band integration. The case studies include angular emissivity on curved objects, imaging through infrared windows, and reflection-dominated metallic scenes [7].

IR-synth belongs to this family. The main difference is the goal. These projects are research tools built for one experiment each. I wanted a general add-on that lives inside Blender's interface, can be driven from scripts, and treats the temperature field as something you edit and randomize.

Learned translation from RGB

The third family skips simulation. It trains a GAN or a diffusion model to turn an RGB image into a thermal image. This can use the large amount of RGB data that already exists. A benchmark on an aerial RGB-thermal dataset found that paired GANs gave the best results, but that the translation still needs improvement before it can be a reliable source of thermal training data [8]. Newer work adds physics to the model. The PID model splits a thermal image into temperature, emissivity, and texture, and uses physical constraints during training [9]. TherA, from CVPR 2026, conditions a diffusion model on a thermal-aware embedding, so that users can control the time of day, the weather, and the state of objects [10].

This approach does help. In one study on vehicle detection from drones, synthetic infrared images from a diffusion model raised the detection score from 50.8 to 60.1 mAP on one target dataset. The authors also report that a gap to real infrared data remains, and that traits you cannot see in RGB, such as engine heat, make the mapping hard to learn [11].

Where this leaves a gap

Learned methods are fast and flexible, but they give no ground truth temperature and they can invent heat that is not there. Full physics simulators are accurate, but they are heavy and hard to reach. Between them there is room for a tool that is open, scriptable, and sits inside a general 3D program. Its temperatures should be explicit numbers that the user controls. IR-synth tries to fill this gap. It does not try to replace a validated signature simulator.

Our approach

The central idea is to split the problem into two parts that do not depend on each other. 

1. temperature field     where is it hot? (Kelvin, per vertex)
2. radiometry            what does a sensor record for that temperature?

The first part is geometry work. It happens once, in NumPy, and the result is written into the mesh as an attribute named thermal_temperature_k. A second attribute, thermal_emissivity, holds the emissivity. Both are one float per vertex. The second part is a shader. It reads the two attributes, applies a transfer function, and lets Cycles do the rest. Because the shader is made of ordinary node math, it runs on the GPU and stays editable in Blender.

This split has a useful consequence. The temperature field can come from anywhere: painted by hand, computed from a formula, or produced by an outside solver. The renderer does not care. It only needs the attribute.

A second decision shapes the whole add-on: the pixel value must stay a measurement. The raw output is a linear signal that can be inverted back to a temperature. False color exists only for looking at the scene in the viewport. I rejected normalizing against the scene minimum and maximum, because it would weld a palette range into the material, make every frame scale to itself, and destroy the link between pixel and temperature.

One more honest note. IR-synth does not solve heat transfer. The temperature field is an initial state. There are field operations such as smoothing and contact diffusion, but the code describes contact diffusion as a static approximation of an already diffused state, not a simulation. Time evolution is a planned feature.

The physics in short

A transfer function maps a surface temperature in Kelvin to a sensor signal. All of them approximate Planck's law for a narrow band around one center wavelength. IR-synth implements three: 

Wien              S(T) = R * exp(-B / T) + O
Rayleigh-Jeans    S(T) = R * T + O
RBFO              S(T) = R / (exp(B / T) - F) + O

Here B = h c / (λ k) for the band center wavelength λ, about 1439 K for 10 micrometers. R absorbs the constants, the band width, and the detector gain, and O is the dark signal offset. RBFO is the form that real long wave cameras use to publish their calibration. The FLIR AX5 documentation gives its constants this way [13]. It keeps the full exponential, so it stays accurate over a wider range than Wien. Wien is better at low temperatures or short wavelengths, and Rayleigh-Jeans at the opposite end. The theory behind these approximations and the notation follow the standard textbook on thermography errors [12].

Every transfer can also run backward. Given a signal, it returns the temperature. This is what makes the raw output a measurement, and it is used to label the legend of the false color display. Two transfers, Stefan-Boltzmann and a band-integrated Planck lookup table, are declared in the code but not implemented yet.

Emissivity enters after the transfer, in signal space and not in temperature space: 

S_apparent = eps * S(T_surface) + (1 - eps) * S(T_reflected)

Some inputs can break these formulas. RBFO with F > 1 has a denominator that reaches zero at T = B / ln(F), and the add-on computes this limit so it can report a bad scene before it renders. Temperatures are clamped to at least 1 K, and the exponent is capped at 80, so bad input cannot produce infinities or NaNs in an image.

Architecture

The add-on is about nine thousand lines of Python, and it is split by one rule: the simulation code never imports bpy. 

ext/
  thermal_core/   pure Python + NumPy, no bpy
                  specs, config, field, pipeline, operations, radiometry
  hooks/          hook registry for outside code, no bpy
  ui/             property groups, panels, spec resolution (bpy)
  operators/      bake, visualize, operation stack, environment (bpy)
  shaders/        node graph builders, thermal world, render settings (bpy)

A test checks this rule. It starts a fresh Python process with no Blender, imports the core, and runs a bake with a source and an operation. If someone adds a bpy import to the core, the test fails. This is what allows continuous integration to run the physics without Blender, and it is what allows a script to build and change a thermal setup without a running Blender session.

The conversion boundary

Blender state and the core meet in one place. The file ui/resolution.py reads live properties and produces frozen dataclasses. For every object it resolves a spec by walking a chain: the object, then each collection that contains it, then the scene default. If an object inherits from two collections whose specs disagree, the resolver raises an error and does not pick one silently.

Geometry crosses the boundary through a class called MeshSampler. It copies vertex positions in world space, the edge list, and optional vertex group weights into a MeshSample made of NumPy arrays. World space is used because operations that read two objects need a shared frame. After evaluation, an AttributeWriter converts the float64 results to float32 and writes them back to the mesh.

Specs, registries, and twins

Every choice a user can make is a small frozen dataclass: UniformTempSpec, GradientTempSpec, WeightPaintedEmissivitySpec, ClampOp, and so on. Each one validates itself and can be written to JSON with its class name and a schema version, then read back. Behavior is attached through registries keyed by enums. To add a temperature strategy, you write the dataclass and one registered function: 

@BakeStrategyRegistry.register(GradientTempSpec)
def _evaluate_gradient(spec, sample):
    point_a = np.asarray(spec.point_a, dtype=np.float64)
    axis = np.asarray(spec.point_b, dtype=np.float64) - point_a
    axis_length_sq = float(np.dot(axis, axis))
    # t = 0 at point_a, t = 1 at point_b, clamped beyond the endpoints
    t = np.clip((sample.positions - point_a) @ axis / axis_length_sq, 0.0, 1.0)
    return spec.value_a_k + t * (spec.value_b_k - spec.value_a_k)

(The real function also checks for a degenerate axis.) Callers never branch on the spec type. They call evaluate() and the registry finds the function.

Most registries have a twin on the Blender side. The numeric registries in the core (BakeStrategyRegistry, OpRegistry, TransferRegistry) are mirrored by registries that know how to draw a panel and build a spec from properties (InitStrategyRegistry, OperationRegistry) or how to build a node graph (TransferNodeRegistry, ShaderRegistry). The transfer functions are the best example. The same formula exists twice, as NumPy code for validation and legends, and as a chain of Math nodes for rendering. The module documentation says plainly that the two must agree. For RBFO, the node chain is: 

max(T, 1)  →  B / T  →  min(., 80)  →  exp  →  - F  →  max(., 1e-6)  →  R / .  →  + O

This is the same guarded computation that the NumPy version performs. A shared design for both is the main defense against a render that silently drifts from the numbers.

Failure handling

The pipeline does not raise on the first problem. It collects Failure objects with a reason (invalid spec, not implemented, missing geometry, operation failed) and continues. A broken operation is skipped and the others still run. At the end, the bake operator reports one line, for example how many objects were baked and how many were skipped for each reason. In a scene with hundreds of objects this is much more useful than a stack trace.

Workflow of the extension

In Blender, the add-on appears as a Thermography tab in the 3D viewport sidebar, plus tabs in the Properties editor for scenes and collections. A normal session goes like this. 

Thermal Environment  →  per-object / per-collection specs  →  operation stack
        →  bake  →  shading settings  →  build thermal material  →  render

1. Describe the environment. In Scene Properties, under Thermal Environment, you add factors. Ambient Temperature is the background of the scene. Default Initialization is the temperature strategy used by objects that inherit. A Humidity factor exists in the type list but is not implemented.

2. Set temperatures and emissivity. The Object panel offers Inherit, Uniform, Ambient, Gradient, and Weight Painted for temperature. A gradient is defined by two points in world space. You can copy each point from the 3D cursor, and an overlay in the viewport draws the two points and an arrow toward the hot end. Weight painted takes a vertex group and remaps its weights onto a temperature range, with a linear or smooth falloff. Emissivity works the same way with Inherit, Uniform, and Weight Painted, and the scene has a default value. Collections have their own tab with the same controls.

3. Shape the field with operations. The operation stack runs after all sources are evaluated, in order. Order matters, because you may want to smooth an object before it diffuses onto another. There are three operations:

  • Clamp keeps values inside a Kelvin range.
  • Smooth runs Laplacian smoothing over the mesh edges. Each pass moves a vertex toward the mean of its neighbors by a given factor.
  • Contact diffusion warms receiver objects near source objects. Each receiver vertex finds its nearest source vertex, and blends toward it with a weight that falls from the chosen strength at zero distance to zero at the radius.

Each operation has a scope: all objects, one collection, or a list of objects. Operations can be muted, duplicated, and reordered. Contact diffusion takes a snapshot of the sources before it writes anything, so the result does not depend on the order in which objects are visited. This is what produces the warm ring on the saucer in the figure above.

4. Bake. One button bakes the temperature and the emissivity together, so the two attributes cannot go out of sync. The panel shows how many mesh objects are ready before you press it. The flow inside is: 

bpy scene
  → ConfigBuilder          resolve specs, build SceneThermalConfig
  → MeshSampler            geometry → MeshSample (NumPy)
  → BakingPipeline         evaluate sources, then fold the operation stack
  → FieldSet               one Kelvin array per object
  → AttributeWriter        float32 mesh attributes

5. Choose how to see it. The Thermography panel has the shading path, the transfer function and its parameters, the emissivity, and the output mode. The button Build Thermal Material creates one shared material, assigns it to every baked mesh, and you look at the result in Material Preview or Rendered mode. A color bar can be drawn in the viewport, and a Fit Span button sets the display range from the baked data.

The three shading paths

Shading types are chosen from a registry, and a type with no implementation says so instead of falling back to something else. Three are implemented today.

  • Temperature Shader. Shows the baked Kelvin values directly, with no radiometry. It is a diagnostic view of what the bake produced.
  • Node Surrogate Reflection. Applies the transfer to the surface and to a constant reflected temperature, and mixes them by emissivity with Math nodes. There is no light transport, so it is fast and the environment is a single number.
  • Ray Node Shader. The surface emits eps * S(T) and reflects with albedo 1 - eps, through a mix of diffuse and glossy lobes controlled by a specular fraction and a roughness. Cycles then carries the emission of other objects into the reflections. The add-on also builds a thermal World whose background has the signal of the ambient temperature, so empty space reflects it.

The ray path needs care, because a normal Blender render changes pixel values to look good. When you build the material in this mode, the add-on sets Cycles as the engine, the Standard view transform, no look, exposure 0, gamma 1, no sample clamping, no glossy filter, no denoising, and at least eight bounces. In a closed space the missing energy is about (1 - eps)^(bounces + 1), so a low emissivity needs more bounces. The add-on also warns when a visible light is left on, because it adds radiance that does not belong in a thermal image, and when a mesh is not baked, because other surfaces would reflect its ordinary material.

The detector offset O is left out of the ray-traced path on purpose. Without it, the signal is linear in radiance, so Cycles can scale and sum it over bounces correctly. The offset is a detector term and should be added once after the render. The panel tells the user that pixels hold S - O, and that the temperature inversion works on that value. For the same reason, false color is blocked in this mode, since other surfaces would reflect the palette.

Conclusion

Blender can render thermal images if temperature is made a first-class quantity and the renderer is stopped from changing the values. IR-synth does this in three steps. It bakes a Kelvin field onto the meshes, converts it with a transfer function that is written twice (NumPy and nodes), and renders reflections with Cycles under settings that keep pixels linear. The structure matters as much as the physics: a core with no Blender dependency, specs that are plain data, registries for every choice, and failures that are reported instead of thrown. The code is open, and contributions are welcome at the repository [14].
